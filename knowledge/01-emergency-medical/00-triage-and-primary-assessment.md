# Triage and Primary Assessment

When there is no ambulance coming, a fast, methodical assessment saves more lives than any single skill. Make the scene safe first: a dead rescuer helps no one.

## Scene safety

- Look for ongoing threats: fire, collapsing structures, downed power lines, traffic, gas smell, armed people, rising water, animals.
- Wear gloves or improvised barriers (plastic bags) against blood.
- Move a casualty only if the scene is unsafe or you must reposition to treat. Suspect spinal injury after falls, vehicle crashes, or diving.

## MARCH sequence (trauma)

Treat in this order. Do not move on until the current step is controlled.

1. **M – Massive hemorrhage.** Spurting or pooling blood kills in 3–5 minutes. Tourniquet limbs; pack and press junctional wounds. See *Controlling Severe Bleeding*.
2. **A – Airway.** Is the person talking? If unconscious: head-tilt/chin-lift (jaw-thrust if spine suspected), clear the mouth, place in recovery position if breathing.
3. **R – Respiration.** Look, listen, feel for 10 seconds. Look for chest wounds; seal open chest wounds with plastic taped on all sides (or a vented seal) and watch for worsening breathing — if it worsens, lift one edge to let air escape.
4. **C – Circulation.** Check pulse (wrist, then neck), skin color/temperature, capillary refill (press nail bed; color should return in under 2 seconds). Look for other bleeding. Lay flat and raise legs if in shock (unless head/chest injury).
5. **H – Hypothermia / Head injury.** Insulate from the ground, cover, keep dry. Every trauma patient gets cold; cold blood does not clot well. Check level of consciousness with AVPU.

## AVPU consciousness scale

- **A**lert – eyes open, knows name/place/time.
- **V**oice – responds to voice.
- **P**ain – responds only to pinch/pressure.
- **U**nresponsive.

Recheck every 15 minutes and write it down. A declining level is an emergency (head bleed, shock, poisoning).

## Mass-casualty triage (START-style)

With many casualties and few helpers, sort fast (about 30 seconds each):

- **Walking wounded → GREEN (minor).** Ask everyone who can walk to move to one spot. Use them as helpers.
- **Not breathing after airway opened → BLACK (expectant/dead).** A hard choice, but resources go to the savable.
- **Breathing over 30/min, no radial pulse or capillary refill over 2 s, or cannot follow simple commands → RED (immediate).**
- **Everyone else → YELLOW (delayed).**

Only quick lifesaving actions during triage: open the airway, apply a tourniquet. Re-triage often; people change categories.

## Secondary survey (head-to-toe)

Once life threats are controlled, check head to toe with your hands: scalp, ears/nose (clear fluid can mean skull fracture), neck, collarbones, chest, abdomen (rigid or swelling = internal bleeding), pelvis (do not rock it), each limb (deformity, pulse, sensation, movement), back.

Record a **SAMPLE** history: Symptoms, Allergies, Medications, Past history, Last food/drink, Events leading up.

## Vital signs to log

Time, pulse rate, breathing rate, AVPU, skin, pain, urine output. Normal adult resting: pulse 60–100, breathing 12–20. Trends matter more than single numbers.

## When field care is not enough

Seek any available doctor or hospital urgently for: uncontrolled bleeding, suspected internal bleeding, chest pain, difficulty breathing, decreasing consciousness, severe burns, open fractures, abdominal pain with a rigid belly, signs of sepsis, or a pregnancy complication.
