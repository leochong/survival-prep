# Wound Care, Closure, and Infection Management

In a low-resource setting, infection kills more slowly than bleeding but just as surely. Cleaning is the single most important step.

## Cleaning (irrigation)

1. Control bleeding first.
2. Wash your hands; use gloves or clean plastic bags.
3. Irrigate with large volumes of **drinkable** water — at least 1 liter for a small laceration, more for dirty wounds. Pressure matters: use a clean plastic bag with a pinhole, or a syringe, squirting from 2–3 inches away.
4. Remove visible dirt with clean tweezers (boil them 10 minutes first).
5. Clean the skin around the wound with soap and water. Avoid repeatedly pouring hydrogen peroxide, iodine tincture, or alcohol into wounds; they damage tissue. Dilute povidone-iodine (weak-tea color, roughly 1%) is acceptable for irrigation.

## To close or not to close

**Leave open (pack loosely and dress) if:**
- Animal or human bite (except possibly the face)
- Puncture wounds
- Heavily contaminated wounds (soil, manure, crush injuries)
- Older than about 12 hours (24 hours on face/scalp)
- Signs of infection already present

**Can close if** clean, fresh, and well irrigated. Options in order of simplicity:
- **Wound-closure strips / butterfly bandages** made from tape for small, low-tension cuts.
- **Hair-tying** for the scalp: twist small bundles of hair from each side, tie, secure.
- **Sutures/staples** only if trained; closing a dirty wound traps infection and can create an abscess.

**Delayed primary closure:** leave a contaminated wound open, packed with moist clean gauze, changed daily; if clean at day 3–5, close then.

## Dressing

- Cover with clean non-stick material (boiled and dried cloth works), then an absorbent layer, then wrap.
- Change daily or when wet/dirty. A thin layer of honey (medical-grade preferred; raw honey is a traditional option) or petroleum jelly keeps it moist and reduces sticking. Do not give honey by mouth to infants under 1 year.

## Signs of infection

Local: increasing redness spreading outward, warmth, swelling, pus, pain that worsens after day 2, red streaks running up the limb (lymphangitis), foul smell.

**Systemic (sepsis) — life-threatening:** fever or low temperature, fast heart rate (over 90), fast breathing (over 20), confusion, very low urine output, mottled skin. Sepsis needs antibiotics and medical care; mortality is high without them.

## Managing infection without a hospital

- Reopen a closed wound if it becomes infected; let pus drain.
- **Abscess:** warm compresses 4–6 times daily. A mature fluctuant (soft, fluid-filled) abscess may need incision and drainage by the most experienced person available, then packing.
- Soak infected hands/feet in warm, clean, salted water (about 1 teaspoon salt per pint) several times daily.
- Mark the edge of redness with a pen and note the time; spreading past the line within hours is an emergency.
- Elevate the limb, rest it, hydrate.
- Antibiotics: use only under a medical professional's guidance or the product's labeled instructions. Never use expired tetracycline-class drugs. Avoid fish/veterinary antibiotics unless no human alternative exists and you truly understand drug choice and dosing — the wrong drug or dose gives false confidence and breeds resistance.

## Tetanus

Highest risk with deep, dirty puncture wounds and wounds contaminated with soil or manure. Symptoms (3–21 days later): jaw stiffness ("lockjaw"), difficulty swallowing, muscle spasms. Keep everyone's tetanus vaccination current *before* a crisis (booster every 10 years). In the field, prevention is thorough cleaning and leaving dirty wounds open.

## Bites

- **Animal bites:** irrigate aggressively with soap and water for 15 minutes; leave open. Any mammal bite carries rabies risk (in the US: bats, raccoons, skunks, foxes, coyotes most often). Rabies is nearly always fatal once symptoms begin; seek post-exposure vaccine if it exists anywhere reachable. Bat contact while asleep counts as exposure.
- **Human bites:** very high infection risk; leave open.
