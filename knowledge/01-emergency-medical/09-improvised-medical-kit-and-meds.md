# Low-Resource Medical Kit, Sterilization, and Medication Basics

## Core kit (stock before a crisis)

**Trauma:** 2+ commercial windlass tourniquets per person, compressed gauze and hemostatic gauze, elastic pressure bandages, chest seals, nitrile gloves, trauma shears.

**Wounds:** sterile gauze, non-stick pads, medical tape, wound-closure strips, povidone-iodine, saline or irrigation syringe, antibiotic ointment, tweezers, magnifier.

**Ortho:** moldable aluminum splint, triangular bandages, elastic wraps.

**Medications:** ibuprofen, acetaminophen, diphenhydramine, loratadine, loperamide, oral rehydration salts, hydrocortisone cream, antifungal cream, epinephrine auto-injector (prescription), naloxone, aspirin (suspected heart attack: 325 mg chewed if not allergic or bleeding), personal prescriptions with as many months of supply as you can legally obtain.

**Tools:** headlamp, thermometer, blood-pressure cuff, pulse oximeter, notebook and pencil.

**Reference:** a printed copy of *Where There Is No Doctor* and *Where There Is No Dentist* (both free PDFs from Hesperian).

## Improvised supplies

- **Gauze:** cotton cloth boiled 20 minutes, dried in sun, stored in a clean sealed bag.
- **Bandages:** strips of cotton sheets; T-shirts.
- **Splints:** see *Fractures, Sprains, and Dislocations*.
- **Irrigation:** bag with a pinhole; clean squeeze bottle.
- **Tape alternatives:** strips of cloth tied; duct tape over cloth (never directly on wounds).
- **Sling:** shirt with the bottom folded up and pinned; a belt.

## Sterilizing instruments

1. Scrub off all visible dirt with soap and water first — sterilization fails on dirty tools.
2. **Boil** in a covered pot for 20 minutes (at a full rolling boil). This kills most organisms but not all spores; it is the best practical option.
3. **Pressure cooker:** 15 psi for 30 minutes approximates an autoclave — the best field method.
4. Flame (holding a needle in a flame until red) works for single small instruments.
5. Chemical soak: 1:10 household bleach (about 0.5% chlorine) for 30 minutes, then rinse with boiled water. Corrodes metal over time.
6. Store sterilized items wrapped in boiled cloth or a sealed bag.

## Pain and fever (adult over-the-counter dosing, per label)

- **Acetaminophen (paracetamol):** 500–1000 mg every 6 hours; no more than 3000 mg/day in an unsupervised setting. Liver damage with overdose or heavy alcohol use.
- **Ibuprofen:** 200–400 mg every 6–8 hours with food; max 1200 mg/day over the counter. Avoid with kidney disease, stomach ulcers, late pregnancy, dehydration, or bleeding.
- Alternating the two gives better pain control than either alone.
- Children: dose by weight per the package; never give aspirin to children or teens (Reye syndrome).

## Medication expiration

- Most solid tablets stored cool, dry, and dark retain most potency for years past the printed date (US military shelf-life testing). Potency declines; it rarely becomes toxic.
- **Do not use when expired:** liquid antibiotics, insulin (keep cold), nitroglycerin, epinephrine (use only if nothing else is available — expired is better than none), eye drops, tetracycline-class antibiotics that are discolored or degraded.
- Store meds in a cool, dark, dry place in original containers with labels.

## Insulin without refrigeration

Unopened insulin stays usable for about 28 days at room temperature (up to 86°F/30°C). Evaporative cooling (clay pot inside a larger clay pot with wet sand between, covered with a wet cloth, in shade) can keep it several degrees cooler. Never freeze insulin.
