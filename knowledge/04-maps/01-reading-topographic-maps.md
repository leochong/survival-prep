# Reading Topographic Maps

## Margin information

Check before using any map: **scale**, **contour interval** (e.g., 20 ft or 40 ft between lines), **date** (roads and structures change), **datum** (NAD27 vs NAD83/WGS84 — older maps differ by up to ~200 m; set your GPS to match), **magnetic declination diagram**, and the **legend**.

## Colors on USGS maps

- **Brown:** contour lines (elevation)
- **Blue:** water — rivers, lakes, springs, marshes
- **Green:** vegetation (woods); white = open areas
- **Black:** man-made features — buildings, trails, railroads, boundaries
- **Red:** major roads, survey grid
- **Purple (older maps):** revisions from aerial photos

## Contour lines

Each line connects points of equal elevation. Every fifth line (index contour) is darker and labeled.

- **Close together** = steep; **far apart** = gentle; touching lines = cliff.
- **Hill/summit:** concentric closed circles.
- **Valley/drainage:** contour lines form **V's or U's pointing uphill** (upstream). Streams run along these.
- **Ridge/spur:** V's or U's pointing **downhill**.
- **Saddle:** a low point between two hilltops — an hourglass shape. Common route over ridges.
- **Depression:** closed circles with tick marks (hachures) pointing inward.

**Rule of V's:** when contour lines cross a stream, they point upstream.

## The five major and three minor terrain features

Major: hill, ridge, valley, saddle, depression. Minor: draw (small valley), spur (small ridge), cliff. Learn to see them in 3D from contour patterns — this is terrain association, the most reliable navigation skill.

## Measuring distance

- Use the bar scale: mark the distance on paper's edge and compare to the scale.
- For winding routes, lay a string along the route, then straighten it on the scale.
- Add 10–20% for real-world wiggles; add much more for steep terrain (see *Naismith's rule* in navigation).

## Determining elevation and slope

- Find the nearest index contour, count lines up or down × contour interval.
- Slope (%) = rise ÷ run × 100. Over 30% is hard going on foot; over 45° (100%) needs climbing.

## Planning a route

- Follow handrails (linear features: streams, ridges, roads, power lines).
- Pick catching features (a road or river beyond your target that stops you if you overshoot).
- Use attack points (an easy-to-find feature near the target) for the final approach.
- Avoid crossing many contour lines; contouring around a hill can be faster than going over it.
- Note water, escape routes, and bailout points.
