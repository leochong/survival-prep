# Map and Compass Navigation

A baseplate compass (with a rotating bezel, orienting arrow, and direction-of-travel arrow) and a topo map are the core kit. GPS is great until batteries die or signals are disrupted.

## Magnetic declination

A compass points to magnetic north, not true (map) north. The difference is **declination**, and in the contiguous US it ranges from roughly **15° East in the Pacific Northwest** to **about 14° West in Maine**, near zero along a line through the central US (roughly the Mississippi Valley up through Minnesota). Alaska varies widely (often 15–20°+ East); Hawaii is about 9° East. It changes over the years — check the map diagram and note its date, or a current NOAA value.

- **East declination** (West US): magnetic north is east of true north. To convert a **map (true) bearing to a compass (magnetic) bearing, subtract** east declination. Magnetic → true: add.
- **West declination** (East US): **add** west declination to a true bearing to get magnetic. Magnetic → true: subtract.
- Memory aid: "**East is least, West is best**" (for converting true → magnetic: subtract east, add west).
- Easiest: buy a compass with **adjustable declination** and set it once.

## Taking a bearing from the map

1. Place the compass edge on the map connecting your position to the destination, direction-of-travel arrow pointing toward the destination.
2. Rotate the bezel until its orienting lines are parallel to the map's north-south grid lines, with the bezel N pointing to map north.
3. Read the bearing at the index line. Adjust for declination (if not built in).
4. Hold the compass flat in front of you, turn your body until the red needle sits inside the orienting arrow ("red in the shed"). Walk where the direction-of-travel arrow points.

## Walking a bearing

- Sight on a distant landmark along the bearing, walk to it, repeat.
- Keep metal (vehicles, knives, phones, power lines) away from the compass — they deflect the needle.
- **Aiming off:** aim deliberately to one side of a target on a linear feature (e.g., a bridge on a river), so when you hit the feature you know which way to turn.
- **Detour around obstacles** at right angles: count paces for each leg so you can return to the original line (box method).

## Orienting the map

Rotate the map so north on the map points to north on the ground (set the compass to 0°/360° with declination, lay it along a north-south line, turn map and compass together until "red in the shed"). Now features on the map line up with features around you.

## Finding your location (resection / triangulation)

1. Identify two or three landmarks that you can see and find on the map (peaks, towers, the end of a lake), ideally 60–120° apart.
2. Take a compass bearing to each.
3. Convert to a true bearing and compute the **back bearing** (add 180° if less than 180°, subtract 180° if more).
4. Draw a line from each landmark on the map along the back bearing. Where they intersect is your position. Three lines usually form a small triangle — you're inside it.

If you are on a linear feature (trail, river, ridge), a single bearing to one landmark gives your position where the back-bearing line crosses the feature.

## Pace count

- Count every **second** step (each time the same foot strikes). Walk 100 meters on measured flat ground several times and average. Typical: 60–70 pace counts per 100 m.
- Your pace count increases going uphill, in brush, in snow or sand, when tired, and in the dark. Recalibrate on different terrain.
- Use pace beads or knots on a cord: slide one bead every 100 m.

## Dead reckoning

Navigate by known bearing + distance + time from a known point. Record each leg. Errors accumulate (roughly 5–10% of distance), so confirm by terrain features whenever possible.

## Travel time (Naismith's rule)

Allow 1 hour per 3 miles (5 km) on flat ground, plus 1 hour per 2,000 ft (600 m) of ascent. Add time for loads, rough terrain, night, and group size. Rest 10 minutes each hour.

## When lost

S.T.O.P. (see *Survival Priorities*). Retrace to your last known point if possible. Head toward a catching feature (a road, river, or ridgeline). Following a drainage downhill usually leads to water and people — but avoid canyons with cliffs and waterfalls. If others will search for you, stay put.
