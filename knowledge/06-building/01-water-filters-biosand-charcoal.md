# Building Water Filters

## Quick emergency filter (layered)

Use a cut-off plastic bottle (upside down, cap with small holes), a bucket with holes, or a cloth tube on a tripod. Top to bottom:

1. Cloth pre-filter
2. Fine sand (clean, washed) — 4+ inches
3. Crushed charcoal (from your fire, hardwood, no ash) — 2–3 inches
4. Fine sand
5. Small gravel
6. Coarse gravel
7. Cloth/grass over the outlet

Pour water slowly; discard the first few liters (they'll be dark from charcoal dust). **Then boil or chlorinate.**

## Biosand filter (household, long-term)

A slow sand filter where a biological layer (*schmutzdecke*) forms on the sand surface and removes 90–99% of bacteria and most protozoa once mature (about 2–3 weeks). Treats 20–60 liters per day for a family.

### Container
A food-grade plastic barrel (about 30 gallons) or a concrete box about 3 ft tall.

### Plumbing
An outlet tube from the bottom of the container runs **up the outside** and exits **about 2 inches (5 cm) above the top of the sand**. This keeps the sand bed permanently submerged under a shallow water layer so the biolayer survives.

### Layers (bottom to top)
1. **Drainage gravel** (½ inch / 12 mm): 2 inches (5 cm), covering the outlet tube intake (screen it).
2. **Separating gravel** (¼ inch / 6 mm): 2 inches.
3. **Filter sand** (washed, sieved under 0.7 mm — fine, like table salt): about 21 inches (54 cm). Wash until rinse water is nearly clear. Use crushed rock sand if possible (riverbed sand may contain organics).
4. **Diffuser plate** above the sand: a lid or plate with many small holes, so poured water doesn't dig into the sand.
5. Lid on top.

### Use
- Pour water in once or twice daily; the same amount drips from the outlet.
- Keep water depth at about 2 inches over the sand at rest — never let it dry out.
- Flow rate should be about 0.4 liters/minute when full; if it slows a lot, gently swirl the top inch of sand ("wet harrowing") and scoop off the dirty water.
- Use consistent water sources (sudden changes upset the biolayer).
- **Disinfect output** (chlorine/boiling) for full safety.

## Activated charcoal (improving charcoal for filters)

Regular charcoal works; activated charcoal adsorbs more chemicals and bad tastes. See *Low-Resource Chemistry: Charcoal* for making charcoal. Simple activation: heat crushed charcoal hot in a closed container, then quench it, or soak it in a strong calcium chloride or lemon juice solution, dry, and reheat to drive off moisture. Replace filter charcoal every few months; it becomes saturated.

## Ceramic pot filter

A clay pot fired with sawdust or rice husks mixed into the clay (they burn out, leaving tiny pores). Commercial versions are coated in colloidal silver. Homemade versions are hard to make reliably — treat output.

## What filters can't remove

Dissolved chemicals (nitrates, pesticides, fuel), salts, and heavy metals pass through sand filters. Charcoal removes some organic chemicals. Only distillation removes salts and most metals.
