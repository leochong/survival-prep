# Rocket Stoves, Haybox Cookers, and Solar Ovens

Fuel gathering becomes a major daily labor without utilities. These designs cut fuel use by half or more.

## Rocket stove principles

- Burns small sticks completely and hot, with little smoke.
- **Insulated combustion chamber and chimney** (L- or J-shaped) creates a strong draft.
- Fuel enters from the side and burns at the tips; push sticks in as they burn.
- Air enters under the fuel (a shelf in the fuel opening).
- Pot sits on supports with a small gap (about ½ inch) so hot gas scrubs the pot's sides; a skirt around the pot increases heat transfer.

## Build: tin-can rocket stove

Materials: one large can (#10 / 1-gallon), one smaller can (soup) for the chimney, one can for the fuel feed, insulation (wood ash, perlite, vermiculite, or crushed pumice — **not** sand, which is a poor insulator, and not plastic/foam).

1. Cut a hole in the side of the large can near the bottom, sized for the feed tube.
2. Make an L: cut a hole in the side of the chimney can near its bottom; insert the feed can at an angle or horizontally to form an elbow; seal joints with wire or by folding tabs.
3. Place the L inside the large can, feed tube poking out of the side hole.
4. Pack insulation around the chimney, filling the large can.
5. Make a lid ring for the top that covers the insulation but leaves the chimney open.
6. Add pot supports (bent metal or three bolts) above the chimney.
7. Insert a small metal shelf in the feed tube to let air under the sticks.

## Build: brick/block rocket stove (no tools)

Stack 16–24 bricks or cinderblocks into an L-shaped tunnel — a horizontal feed about 2 bricks long, then a vertical chimney about 4 courses high. Set a grill on top. Not insulated (bricks absorb heat), so less efficient than a tin-can design, but fast.

## Haybox (retained-heat) cooker

Bring food to a boil for a few minutes on the stove, then put the covered pot into a heavily insulated box. It continues cooking without fuel. Saves 50–70% of fuel for rice, beans, stews, grains.

- Box: cooler, wooden crate, cardboard box, or a hole in the ground.
- Insulation: hay, straw, wool blankets, sleeping bags, crumpled newspaper, shredded foam — at least 3–4 inches around all sides including the lid.
- Times: rice 1–2 hours, beans (pre-soaked, boiled 10 minutes first) 3–4 hours, stews 2–3 hours.
- **Food safety:** keep food above 140°F (60°C). Reheat to boiling before serving if it has cooled. Do not leave food in the box overnight.
- **Dry beans:** kidney beans must be boiled hard for at least 10 minutes first (destroys lectins) — slow cooking alone can make them more toxic.

## Solar cookers

- **Box oven:** an insulated box painted black inside with a glass or clear plastic lid and reflectors (foil-covered cardboard) angled toward the sun. Reaches 250–350°F (120–175°C). Cooks like a slow cooker.
- **Panel cooker (CooKit style):** foil-covered cardboard panels reflecting onto a dark pot inside a clear plastic oven bag.
- **Parabolic:** fast and hot, like a stovetop; can cause eye injury and burns.
- Works best from about 10 a.m. to 2 p.m. with clear skies. Use thin, dark, lidded pots. Also pasteurizes water: water held at 149°F (65°C) for 6 minutes is pasteurized; a WAPI (water pasteurization indicator) — a wax pellet that melts at about 158°F — confirms it.

## Rocket mass heater (home heating)

A rocket stove whose exhaust runs through a long horizontal duct buried in a cob or masonry bench, storing heat that radiates for hours. Very fuel-efficient. Building one requires care: proper sizing, a heat riser, and draft testing. Have CO alarms; never operate with an inadequate chimney. Follow established plans (e.g., Ianto Evans and Leslie Jackson, *Rocket Mass Heaters*).
