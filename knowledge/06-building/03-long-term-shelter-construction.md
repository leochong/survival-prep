# Long-Term Shelter Construction

Choose a technique that matches your local materials and climate.

## Site and foundation basics

- Build on high, well-drained ground; dig a small test pit to see the soil and water table.
- South-facing (in the US) for winter sun; windbreak on the north/west.
- Foundations keep walls dry: rubble trench (a trench filled with gravel, draining to daylight), stone, or concrete. Raise wooden sills at least 8 inches above the ground.
- "Good hat, good boots": wide roof overhangs (2 ft+) and a raised foundation protect every natural material.

## Log cabin

- **Logs:** straight conifers (pine, spruce, fir, cedar, larch) 8–12 inches in diameter. Fell in winter when sap is low; peel bark to slow rot and insects; season if time allows (green logs shrink and settle, so leave gaps above windows and doors).
- **Corners:** saddle notch (simplest — a rounded notch on the underside of each log over the one below) or dovetail.
- **Chinking:** fill gaps with moss, then mud/clay mixed with straw, or lime mortar.
- **Roof:** pole rafters with a steep pitch to shed snow (at least 6:12 where snowy). Covering: split shakes, sod over bark and a waterproof membrane, or metal salvaged from buildings.
- Two people with axes, a saw, and simple levers can build a small (12×14 ft) cabin in a few months.

## Earthbag building

Polypropylene bags (or tubes) filled with moist earth, stacked in courses, tamped flat, with two strands of barbed wire between courses (prevents slipping).
- Soil: slightly moist, ideally 20–30% clay. Gravel-filled bags for the first courses (stop wicking moisture).
- Walls: domes (corbelled — each course stepped slightly inward) or straight walls with buttresses.
- Must be plastered (earth or lime) to protect bags from UV, which destroys polypropylene in months.
- Excellent thermal mass and resistant to fire and bullets; good in arid and seismic regions when designed properly.

## Dugout / earth-sheltered shelter

- Dig into a south-facing slope; earth on three sides moderates temperatures (ground at 5+ ft is near the local average annual temperature).
- Requires excellent drainage — French drains around the outside, and waterproofing (heavy plastic or clay) behind walls.
- The roof must support heavy soil loads (wet earth: about 100+ lb per cubic foot) — use heavy timbers on close spacing. Undersized roof beams in earth shelters can collapse.
- Ventilation is essential (dampness, CO from heating).

## Cob and adobe

- **Cob:** clay subsoil + sand + straw, mixed with feet on a tarp, stacked by hand into monolithic walls. Ratio roughly 1 part clay soil to 1–3 parts sand (test bricks first).
- **Adobe:** the same mix formed into sun-dried bricks in wooden molds; laid with mud mortar. Best in dry climates; vulnerable in earthquakes without reinforcement.
- **Test the soil:** fill a jar one-third with soil, top up with water, shake, and let it settle. Layers show sand (bottom), silt, and clay (top).

## Insulation

Natural materials: straw (dry, as bales or light clay-straw), wool, cellulose, sawdust mixed with lime, wood chips, cattail fluff, dry leaves in sealed cavities. Insulate the ceiling first (heat rises), then walls and floor. Double windows: plastic sheeting over window frames on both sides creates an insulating air gap.

## Ventilation and heating

Every enclosed living space with combustion needs a proper chimney and a fresh-air inlet. Clean chimneys regularly — creosote fires are common in wood-heated homes. Keep a bucket of sand and a shovel by the stove.
