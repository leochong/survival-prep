# Root Cellars and Non-Electric Cold Storage

## Root cellar

A root cellar holds produce at about **32–40°F (0–4°C)** and **85–95% humidity**, using the earth's stable temperature.

### Designs
- **Basement corner:** wall off a corner on the north/east side of an unheated basement; insulate interior walls and ceiling; add an outside window or two vents.
- **Dug into a hillside:** a north-facing slope, at least 3–4 feet of earth cover on the roof, with a door facing north (away from sun).
- **Buried container:** a clean metal trash can or food-grade barrel buried at an angle with the lid at ground level, covered with straw bales and a tarp. Good for small harvests.
- **Clamps / pits:** a straw-lined pit in the garden holding root vegetables, covered with straw and soil. Traditional and cheap.

### Ventilation
Two vents — a low cold-air intake and a high warm-air exhaust on the opposite side — create airflow that removes ethylene gas and excess heat. Screen against rodents.

### What to store and how
- **Cold and moist (32–40°F, 90–95%):** carrots, beets, turnips, parsnips, potatoes (keep dark — green potatoes are toxic), cabbage, apples (store apples away from other produce; they release ethylene).
- **Cool and dry (50–60°F, 60–70%):** winter squash, pumpkins, sweet potatoes (cure them warm first).
- **Cold and dry (32–40°F, 60–70%):** onions, garlic (cure first).
- Store only unblemished produce; check weekly and remove anything rotting. "One bad apple" is literal.
- Pack root crops in damp sand, sawdust, or leaves in boxes.

## Zeer pot (evaporative cooler) — hot, dry climates

1. Place a smaller unglazed clay pot inside a larger one.
2. Fill the gap with sand and keep the sand wet.
3. Cover with a wet cloth. Keep in shade with airflow.
Evaporation can cool contents by 15–25°F (8–14°C) in low humidity. Works poorly in humid climates. Extends fresh produce life from days to weeks.

## Springhouse and stream cooling

A stone or wooden structure built over a cold spring or stream. Sealed containers sit in the flowing water (usually 45–55°F). Protect from animals; use only sealed containers to avoid contamination.

## Ice house

Before refrigeration, ice cut from frozen lakes in winter was stored in thick-walled, well-drained insulated buildings packed with sawdust (12+ inches around the ice). It can last into late summer in northern states. Requires reliable winter freezing, cutting tools, and a lot of sawdust.

## Winter natural refrigeration

In cold climates, an unheated porch, a box outside a north-facing window, or burying in snow keeps food cold. Protect from animals and from repeated freeze-thaw.
