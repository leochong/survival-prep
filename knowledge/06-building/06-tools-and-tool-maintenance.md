# Hand Tools

When power tools stop, hand tools keep working for generations if maintained.

## Core hand-tool kit

**Wood:** felling axe, splitting maul, hatchet, bow saw and pruning saw, crosscut saw (two-person), hand saw (crosscut and rip), brace and bits / hand drill, chisels, drawknife, mallet, wedges.
**Earth:** round-point shovel, mattock/pick, digging bar, garden hoe, rake, wheelbarrow.
**Metal/general:** hammer, files (mill bastard file for sharpening), hacksaw, vise, pliers, wrenches, screwdrivers, tin snips, bolt cutters.
**Measuring:** tape measure, carpenter's square, level, chalk line, plumb bob (weight on a string).
**Fasteners:** a stockpile of nails, screws, bolts, wire, and baling twine — they are hard to make.

## Sharpening

- **Knives:** hold a consistent angle (about 15–20° per side). Coarse stone first until a burr forms along the entire edge, then flip; progress to finer stones; finish by stropping on leather or cardboard. Improvised stones: the unglazed ring on the bottom of a ceramic mug, a smooth river stone, a car window's edge.
- **Axes:** file a convex edge with a mill file (push the file into the edge, wearing gloves), then hone with a puck stone in small circles.
- **Saws:** bow-saw blades are usually replaced; crosscut saws need a saw set and file — learn this skill if you rely on them.
- **Shovels and hoes:** file to a bevel — a sharp shovel digs much faster.

## Safe axe use

- Clear a full swing radius of brush and branches overhead; no bystanders within two axe lengths.
- Split on a chopping block, not the ground; aim for the far side of the round; keep feet wide.
- Glancing blows cause most axe injuries — chop at an angle into the wood, and keep your legs out of the swing path.
- Replace cracked handles; soak loose heads in water briefly or drive in a new wedge.

## Felling small trees (overview)

1. Check for lean, dead branches ("widowmakers"), and two escape routes at 45° back from the fall direction.
2. Cut a notch (face cut) about one-third into the tree on the side facing the fall direction.
3. Make the back cut from the opposite side, slightly above the bottom of the notch, leaving a hinge of uncut wood that steers the fall.
4. Shout a warning and step away along an escape route as it begins to fall. Never stand directly behind the stump (kickback).
Large trees and leaning or hung-up trees are extremely dangerous — leave them to experienced sawyers.

## Improvised tools

- **Digging stick:** hardwood sharpened and fire-hardened (char lightly, scrape, repeat).
- **Stone tools:** flint, chert, obsidian, and quartzite break with sharp conchoidal fractures; strike with a hammerstone at the edge to knap flakes — sharper than steel, but brittle. Wear eye protection.
- **Hafting:** split a handle, insert the head, lash with sinew or cordage, seal with pine-pitch glue (see *Low-Resource Chemistry*).
- **Spring-pole lathe, pole-lathe, shaving horse:** classic human-powered woodworking setups from scrap wood.
- **Salvage:** leaf springs from vehicles make excellent knives and chisels (high-carbon steel); old files make knives (anneal, shape, re-harden, and temper).

## Basic blacksmithing (orientation)

A forge can be a hole lined with clay, fed by a blower (hair dryer, bellows, hand-crank), burning charcoal or coal. Steel glows: dull red ~1,100°F; cherry red ~1,500°F (forging). Hardening: heat high-carbon steel until a magnet no longer sticks, quench in warm oil. Tempering: reheat to a straw color (~400–450°F) to reduce brittleness. Use tongs, leather apron, eye protection; quench oil can flash fire — keep a lid handy.
