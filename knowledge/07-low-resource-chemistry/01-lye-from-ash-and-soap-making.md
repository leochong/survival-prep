# Making Lye from Wood Ash and Making Soap

Soap is one of the most important disease-prevention tools. It can be made from two things a homestead produces: hardwood ashes and animal fat.

## Part 1: Leaching lye (potash) from ashes

Hardwood ash (oak, maple, hickory, beech, fruitwoods) works best. Softwood (pine) ash is weaker. Ash must be **fully burned (white/gray), dry**, and free of trash, treated wood, or coal ash.

### The ash hopper / barrel
1. Drill several small holes in the bottom of a wooden barrel or plastic bucket (no aluminum). Set it on blocks over a collection container (glass, enamel, stainless, or plastic).
2. Inside the bottom, layer: a few inches of clean gravel, then straw or grass as a filter.
3. Fill with ashes, tamping lightly. Leave a depression on top.
4. Slowly pour **rainwater or soft water** (hard water reduces yield) — preferably hot — over the ashes. It may take hours to a day to start dripping out.
5. Collect the brown liquid (lye water / potassium hydroxide and carbonate solution). Run it through again to strengthen it.

### Testing strength (traditional)
- A fresh egg or a small potato **floats** with about a quarter-sized area showing above the surface: strong enough for soap.
- Too weak: boil it down (outdoors — the fumes are caustic) or pass through fresh ashes again.
- A chicken feather dissolving quickly also indicates strong lye.

**Note:** ash lye is mainly potassium-based and makes **soft soap** (a gel or paste), not hard bars. Adding salt during the boil converts some to a harder sodium soap (see below).

## Part 2: Rendering fat

1. Chop fat (beef/mutton = tallow; pork = lard; also bear, deer) into small pieces.
2. Melt slowly over low heat with a little water so it doesn't scorch.
3. Strain through cloth. Cool; scrape the clean fat off the top of the water.
4. Repeat for whiter, less smelly fat.
Vegetable oils work too (olive, sunflower, corn), though they make softer soap.

## Part 3: Soft soap (traditional boiled method)

1. In an iron or stainless kettle outdoors, heat the rendered fat.
2. Gradually add lye water, stirring. Rough starting ratio: **about 1 part fat to 2–3 parts lye water by volume** — adjust as you go.
3. Boil gently, stirring, for several hours. The mixture thickens to a translucent, honey-like paste.
4. **Test:** a spoonful should cool to a clear jelly-like soap.
   - Greasy film or oil puddles → needs more lye.
   - Bites the tongue sharply / feels slippery-burning on skin → too much lye; add more fat and cook longer. (Use the red cabbage indicator instead of the tongue test where possible.)
5. Store in crocks or jars. Use for laundry, dishes, and washing.

### Making it harder (salting out)
Near the end of the boil, stir in salt (roughly 1–2 pints per 5 gallons of soap). Soap curds float to the top; let it cool and skim the hard soap off. Washing the curds in brine and reboiling improves quality.

## Part 4: Bar soap with commercial lye (sodium hydroxide)

If you have pure sodium hydroxide (100% lye, drain-opener grade, no additives):

**Basic tallow/lard recipe (cold process):**
- 32 oz (900 g) lard or tallow
- 4.3 oz (122 g) sodium hydroxide (≈5% superfat for lard; check a lye calculator for other fats)
- 10–12 oz (280–340 g) water

Steps: carefully add lye to cold water outdoors (fumes, heat to ~200°F); let cool to ~100–110°F. Melt fat to ~100–110°F. Pour lye solution into fat, stir until "trace" (pudding thickness, a drizzle leaves a trail). Pour into lined molds, insulate 24–48 hours, cut, cure 4–6 weeks.

Different fats need different amounts of lye — **always use a saponification (SAP) value table or calculator** you have printed in advance. Too much lye makes caustic soap; too little makes greasy soap.

## Uses for lye beyond soap

Nixtamalizing corn (traditionally with wood ash or lime — see *Lime*), curing olives (requires precision), cleaning drains, processing hides (hair removal).
