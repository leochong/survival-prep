# Lime

Lime has been central to building, sanitation, agriculture, and food processing for thousands of years.

## The lime cycle

1. **Limestone, chalk, marble, or shells** (calcium carbonate, CaCO₃)
2. **Burn** at about 900°C / 1,650°F (bright orange-yellow heat) for many hours → **quicklime** (calcium oxide, CaO) + CO₂.
3. **Slake** quicklime with water → **slaked/hydrated lime** (calcium hydroxide, Ca(OH)₂). Gets very hot.
4. **Carbonation:** slaked lime absorbs CO₂ from air over weeks to years → hardens back to limestone. This is how lime mortar sets.

### Testing for limestone
Limestone fizzes when a drop of vinegar (or other acid) is placed on it. Shells (oyster, clam) also work.

## Burning lime (kiln)

- Small-scale: a pit or stone/brick kiln with alternating layers of fuel (wood or charcoal) and broken stone pieces (fist-sized). Burn continuously for 24–72 hours.
- Properly burned lime is lighter than the original stone and crumbles/slakes vigorously when water is added. Underburned cores remain hard.
- Hazardous: extreme heat, CO, and caustic dust. Keep quicklime completely dry until use.

## Slaking

Add quicklime to a large amount of water in a metal tub or pit (not the reverse), outdoors, standing back — it hisses, boils, and spits. Stir with a long hoe. Result: **lime putty**, which improves with age (store under a layer of water for weeks to months; it keeps indefinitely).

## Lime mortar and plaster

- **Mortar:** 1 part lime putty to 2.5–3 parts clean sharp sand. Mix well. Keep the work damp and shaded for several days as it cures (slower than cement but flexible and breathable — ideal for stone, brick, adobe, and log chinking).
- **Plaster/render:** similar mix, often with animal hair or straw fiber for strength, applied in thin coats.
- **Hydraulic lime** (from limestone containing clay) sets even under water.

## Whitewash

Mix slaked lime with water to a milk-like consistency; add a handful of salt per gallon to help it bind. Brush onto walls, barns, fences, and the trunks of fruit trees (prevents sunscald). It is mildly antiseptic — used historically in dairies, chicken coops, and hospitals.

## Sanitation uses

- Sprinkling hydrated lime over latrine contents reduces odor and flies and raises the pH (kills many pathogens).
- Lime treatment of sewage sludge and burial sites was standard historically.
- Use carefully: caustic to skin and eyes.

## Agriculture

Ground limestone or wood ash raises the pH of acidic soils (common in the East and Pacific Northwest). Test soil before applying; too much locks up nutrients.

## Nixtamalization (critical for corn-based diets)

Corn eaten as a staple without nixtamalization causes **pellagra** (niacin deficiency: diarrhea, dermatitis, dementia, death).
1. Simmer dried corn kernels in water with **food-grade slaked lime (cal)** — about 1 tablespoon per pound of corn (≈ 1% by weight) — or with hardwood ash lye (traditional).
2. Simmer 30–60 minutes, then soak overnight.
3. Rinse thoroughly, rubbing off the loosened skins.
4. Grind wet into masa (tortillas, tamales) or dry for hominy/grits.
Use only clean, food-grade lime or clean hardwood ash for food.
