# Glues, Candles, Tanning, Salt, and Vinegar

## Pine pitch glue

Strong, waterproof hafting glue for tools, arrows, and patching containers.
1. Collect hardened pine or spruce resin (sap blobs on wounded trees).
2. Melt slowly near — **not in** — the fire (it is flammable) on a flat stone or in a can.
3. Mix in about **1 part finely crushed charcoal to 3–4 parts resin**, plus a pinch of dried herbivore dung or fine plant fiber as temper.
4. Gather on a stick into a "glue stick." Reheat to apply; it hardens as it cools. Too brittle → add a little fat or beeswax.

## Hide glue

Boil scraps of rawhide, hide, hooves, or fish skins slowly in water for hours until it thickens; strain. Use warm. It sets as it cools and dries — strong for woodworking, but not waterproof.

## Candles and lamps

- **Tallow candles:** dip a cotton wick repeatedly into melted rendered tallow, cooling between dips. Tallow smokes and smells a little; beeswax is far better.
- **Beeswax candles:** melt wax (in a double boiler — it's flammable), dip or pour into molds.
- **Wicks:** braided cotton string soaked in a borax/salt solution and dried burns more cleanly.
- **Oil lamps:** a cotton wick in a jar of vegetable oil, lard, or animal fat. Float a wick on a cork or a tin-can holder. Safe and simple.
- **Rushlights:** peeled rush pith dipped in fat — traditional, about 30 minutes each.
- Fire safety: keep candles in stable holders, away from fabric; never leave burning unattended.

## Tanning hides

### Preparation
1. **Flesh** the hide: scrape off all fat and meat with a dull blade over a smooth log (beam).
2. **Remove the hair (optional):** soak in a wood-ash or lime solution for several days until hair slips, then scrape. Rinse well.
3. Salt-cure (cover the flesh side with salt) if you can't tan immediately.

### Brain tanning (buckskin)
Every animal has enough brain to tan its own hide. Mash the brain into warm water to a creamy emulsion; work it into the hide thoroughly. Wring out, then **stretch and work the hide continuously as it dries** (over a rope, a stake, or by pulling) — this softness step is what makes buckskin. **Smoke** the hide over a smoky, punky-wood fire so it stays soft after getting wet.

### Bark tanning (leather)
Tannin-rich bark: oak, hemlock, chestnut, sumac leaves. Chop/crush and soak in water for weeks to make tan liquor. Soak dehaired hides, increasing the strength over weeks to months until the tan penetrates fully (cut a sliver edge: uniformly colored = done). Produces durable leather for shoes, straps, and harness.

## Salt

- **Sea salt:** evaporate seawater in shallow pans in the sun, or boil it down (seawater is about 3.5% salt — roughly 4–5 oz, or about ½ cup, of salt per gallon). Filter seawater through cloth first. Avoid seawater near outfalls and harbors.
- **Salt springs and licks:** inland brine springs (historically in New York, Ohio Valley, West Virginia, Utah) can be boiled down.
- **Plant ash:** ash of some plants (e.g., coltsfoot, hickory) contains salts — traditional low-sodium salt substitute.
- Salt is critical for food preservation (see *Food Preservation*) and electrolyte balance. Animals need it too.

## Vinegar

1. Make a weak alcoholic liquid: fruit juice, apple cider, or sugar/honey water left to ferment with yeast for 1–3 weeks.
2. Add a "mother" (from raw vinegar) or simply leave uncovered under a cloth (to keep flies out) so acetic acid bacteria colonize it.
3. Wait 3–8 weeks at room temperature; taste periodically.
Homemade vinegar's acidity varies — **do not rely on it for canning or pickling** unless tested (needs ≥ 5% acidity). Use for cleaning, cooking, and refrigerator pickles.

## Natural dyes (bonus)

Walnut husks (brown, no mordant needed), onion skins (yellow/orange), goldenrod (yellow), pokeberry (red-purple, fades; toxic — handle with gloves), indigo (blue). Alum mordant improves colorfastness.
