# Survival Gardening and Seed Saving

A garden takes **months** to produce and a full season to learn — start before you need it.

## Calorie crops first

Most vegetables are low-calorie. To actually feed people, grow:
- **Potatoes** (highest calories per square foot in most climates; plant seed potatoes, hill as they grow)
- **Sweet potatoes** (South, warm regions; leaves are also edible)
- **Winter squash and pumpkins** (store for months)
- **Dry beans** (protein, store for years)
- **Flint/dent corn** (grind for meal; nixtamalize — see *Lime*)
- **Wheat, oats, barley, amaranth** (need more space and threshing labor)
- **Sunflowers** (seeds for oil/protein)
- **Peanuts** (warm South)

Then high-nutrient crops: kale, collards, cabbage, carrots, beets, tomatoes, peppers, onions, garlic, and perennial crops (asparagus, rhubarb, berries, fruit and nut trees).

### Rough area needs
Biointensive methods estimate around **4,000 sq ft (~0.1 acre) per person** for a complete diet in good conditions with skill and compost; conventional row gardening needs much more. Expect lower yields in your first years.

## The Three Sisters

A traditional Indigenous polyculture: corn (stalks support), pole beans (fix nitrogen, climb corn), squash (shades soil, suppresses weeds). Plant corn first; add beans when corn is ~6 inches; squash around the mound.

## Regional notes (USDA zones and seasons)

- **Northeast / Upper Midwest / Rockies (zones 3–6):** short season — choose early varieties, start seeds indoors, use cold frames; potatoes, cabbage family, beans, squash, root crops, oats.
- **Southeast (zones 7–10):** two seasons (spring and fall), heat and pests in summer — sweet potatoes, southern peas (cowpeas), okra, collards, peanuts, corn; grow greens in winter.
- **Great Plains:** wind and drought — windbreaks, mulch, drought-tolerant crops (sorghum, tepary beans, sunflowers, squash, winter wheat).
- **Southwest:** water is everything — sunken waffle gardens, shade cloth, drip irrigation, monsoon-season planting; tepary beans, native corn, squash, amaranth, chiles.
- **Pacific Northwest:** cool summers, wet winters — kale, potatoes, cabbage family, peas, fava beans, garlic; tomatoes struggle.
- **California:** year-round in many zones; summer irrigation essential.
- **Alaska:** long daylight, short season — potatoes, cabbage (giant), root crops, greens, hardy grains; greenhouses/high tunnels.
- **Hawaii:** year-round — sweet potato, taro, breadfruit, banana, cassava (cook properly).

Know your **average last spring frost and first fall frost** dates; most planting schedules hang on them.

## Soil fertility without stores

- **Compost:** 3 parts "brown" (dry leaves, straw, sawdust) to 1 part "green" (food scraps, fresh manure, green plants); keep moist; turn. Hot piles (130–160°F) kill weed seeds and pathogens.
- **Manure:** aged/composted only; chicken manure is strong. Avoid dog, cat, and pig manure on food crops (parasites).
- **Urine:** diluted 1:10, a good nitrogen source for growing plants (not on leafy greens close to harvest).
- **Wood ash:** potassium and lime (raises pH) — use lightly.
- **Cover crops:** clover, vetch, peas, rye fix nitrogen and protect soil.
- **Humanure:** only via long thermophilic composting (a year+) — follow established guides (e.g., Jenkins, *The Humanure Handbook*).

## Water

Mulch heavily (straw, leaves, wood chips) to retain moisture. Water deeply and less often. Drip irrigation from elevated barrels (gravity) saves water. Ollas (buried unglazed clay pots filled with water) irrigate roots slowly.

## Seed saving

- Use **open-pollinated/heirloom** seeds — hybrids (F1) don't breed true.
- **Easiest:** beans, peas, lettuce, tomatoes (self-pollinating). Ferment tomato seeds in water 3 days, rinse, dry.
- **Harder:** squash, corn, cabbage family (cross-pollinate; need isolation distance or hand-pollination; corn needs 200+ plants for genetic diversity).
- Biennials (carrots, beets, cabbage, onions) set seed only in their second year — overwinter them.
- Dry seeds thoroughly; store cool, dark, dry in sealed jars (with silica gel or dry rice). Most last 3–5 years; onions and parsnips only 1–2.
- Keep a seed bank of at least 2–3 years' supply.

## Pests without chemicals

Hand-picking, row covers, crop rotation, companion planting (marigolds, nasturtiums, herbs), chickens/ducks for slugs and insects, soap spray (1 tablespoon pure soap per quart water) for aphids, diatomaceous earth for crawling insects, fencing for deer and rabbits.
