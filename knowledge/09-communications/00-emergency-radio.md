# Emergency Radio Communications

When cell networks fail, radio is the backbone of communication. Know the services, their range, and licensing rules (in normal times, transmitting without the proper license is illegal; in a genuine emergency involving immediate safety of life, FCC rules permit any means of communication).

## Receive-only (no license)

- **NOAA Weather Radio:** 162.400–162.550 MHz (7 channels). Get a battery/hand-crank model with SAME alerts.
- **AM/FM broadcast:** Emergency Alert System messages; AM travels farther at night.
- **Shortwave (HF):** international broadcasts, news when domestic sources are down.
- **Scanner:** monitor local public-safety, aircraft, and other services for situational awareness.

## Two-way services

| Service | License | Typical range | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| **FRS** (Family Radio, 462/467 MHz, 22 channels) | None | 0.5–2 miles (line of sight, more from hills) | Cheap "blister-pack" walkie-talkies. Fixed antennas, 2 W max. |
| **GMRS** (same band as FRS, up to 50 W, repeaters) | FCC license, no test, covers family | 1–5 miles handheld; 20+ miles with repeaters/base | Excellent family/neighborhood option. |
| **MURS** (151–154 MHz, 5 channels) | None | 1–3 miles | Less crowded, VHF. |
| **CB** (27 MHz, 40 channels) | None | 1–5 miles; occasionally far by "skip" | Truckers (Ch 19); **Channel 9 = emergency**. |
| **Amateur (ham) radio** | FCC exam (Technician → General → Extra) | Local to worldwide | Most capable; organized emergency nets (ARES/RACES). |
| **Marine VHF** | Ships/boats | 5–20+ miles on water | **Channel 16 (156.8 MHz)** distress. Not legal on land in normal times. |

## Ham radio essentials

- **Technician license** (35-question multiple-choice exam): VHF/UHF privileges — 2 m (144–148 MHz) and 70 cm (420–450 MHz), repeaters reaching 20–50+ miles.
- **General license:** most HF bands — regional and nationwide contact without any infrastructure.
- Common calling frequencies: **146.520 MHz** (2 m FM simplex national calling), **446.000 MHz** (70 cm simplex). HF: 40 m and 80 m for regional (NVIS) communication; 20 m for long distance in daytime.
- Program local repeaters (and their tones) into your radio in advance; print the list.

## Range tips

- VHF/UHF is line-of-sight: **antenna height is everything.** Getting 30 ft up can double or triple range. Hilltops are best.
- Replace stock "rubber duck" antennas with longer whip antennas; use a roll-up J-pole antenna on a tree.
- Higher power helps less than a better antenna.
- Build a simple dipole for HF: two wires, each roughly 234 ÷ frequency (MHz) feet long, fed in the center with coax.

## Power

Keep spare batteries, AA battery packs for handhelds, a 12 V battery with a solar panel for base stations (see *Off-Grid Solar*). Receiving uses little power; transmitting uses much more — keep transmissions short.

## Protecting electronics (EMP/solar storm)

Store spare radios, a small solar panel, and a charge controller in a Faraday container: a metal trash can or ammo can with a tight lid, the inside lined with cardboard so items don't touch the metal. Effectiveness varies; test by putting a phone inside and calling it (no ring is a good sign).
